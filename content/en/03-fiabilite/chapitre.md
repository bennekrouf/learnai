## A death, a ticket, a chatbot

November 2022. Jake Moffatt learns that his grandmother has died.

He has to fly. He opens Air Canada's website.

He asks the chatbot whether there is a reduced bereavement fare.

The chatbot answers: yes. Buy your ticket now. Ask for a refund of the difference afterwards, within 90 days.

He buys. He asks for the refund.

Air Canada refuses. The real rule is clear: no refunds after travel.

## The link that said the opposite

A cruel detail: the chatbot's answer contained a link.

The link led to the official page on bereavement fares.

And that page said the opposite of the chatbot.

:::pourquoi
The chatbot had the right source at hand.
It cited it.
But its sentence wasn't supported by that source.
Citing a document doesn't prove you say what it says.
:::

## “A separate legal entity”

February 2024. The case comes before a British Columbia tribunal.

Air Canada's defense: the chatbot is a separate legal entity, responsible for its own actions.

The tribunal calls the argument “remarkable”. It rejects it.

The whole website belongs to Air Canada. So does the chatbot.

Air Canada has to pay: a little over 800 Canadian dollars.

:::pourquoi
The sum is small. The principle is huge.
A company answers for what its AI says.
A made-up answer has a legal cost, not just a reputational one.
:::

## The lesson

Retrieving the right document isn't enough.

The answer must also **say what the document says**.

And when the document says nothing, you must know how **not to answer**.

## In this chapter

- **Improving the question**: rewriting, splitting, searching with an imagined answer.
- **Multi-step search**: search, read, search again, and know when to stop.
- **Grounding**: citing sources, and checking that they really say that.
- **Abstention**: confidence thresholds, and the art of saying “I don't know”.
- **Detecting hallucinations**: through consistency, and through a judge.
